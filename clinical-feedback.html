Feedback & Research

A different view of the procedure.

Akoya makes adult and pediatric eyewear designed to limit the view of procedure activity. We believe patients’ visual preferences deserve attention, and we’re interested in how this approach fits everyday clinical care.

Explore the research ↓

The questions behind our approach

Published research and early patient feedback inform the questions we want to explore: what patients prefer to see, how the eyewear feels, and where it fits into a team’s workflow.

The studies below evaluated other approaches, not Akoya products. Neither that research nor our early feedback establishes that Akoya reduces anxiety, pain, or other clinical symptoms.

Published research

Explore the evidence

Open a study for its findings, limitations, and original publication. This is a selected reading list; findings vary by intervention, population, and outcome.

Adults · Vaccination · 2018Looking toward or away from a needlePilot randomized trial · Looking behavior

What was studied: 160 adults receiving influenza vaccinations were assigned to look toward or away from the needle.

What was found: Participants assigned to look away reported less fear. The study did not find evidence of a difference in pain attributable to the looking assignment.

How it relates: This study raises questions about visual exposure during needle procedures. It tested instructions to look away, not eyewear. The authors called for a larger study in a different population.

Mithal et al. Canadian Journal of Pain. 2018;2(1):1–8. DOI: 10.1080/24740527.2017.1412254.

Children ages 6–11 · Dental care · 2018Concealing a dental syringe during children’s injectionsPilot crossover trial · Syringe camouflage

What was studied: 30 children received local anesthesia with conventional and toy-like camouflaged syringes at separate appointments.

What was found: The camouflaged syringe was associated with smaller pulse-rate changes and lower observed behavioral pain scores.

How it relates: The study explores changing the appearance of procedure equipment. Its small sample and combined concealment-and-distraction approach limit the conclusions. It did not test a visual barrier worn by the child or Akoya Pediatric Character Eyewear.

Melwani et al. Journal of Dental Anesthesia and Pain Medicine. 2018;18(1):35–40. DOI: 10.17245/jdapm.2018.18.1.35.

Children ages 6–10 · Dental care · 2023A closer look at syringe camouflageRandomized trial · Mixed findings across outcomes

What was studied: 60 children received dental local anesthesia with a camouflaged or traditional syringe.

What was found: Observed behavioral pain scores were lower with camouflage. Differences in anxiety ratings and heart rate were not statistically significant.

How it relates: This study shows why findings need to be considered outcome by outcome. The needle itself was kept out of sight in both groups; the trial did not isolate the effect of blocking a child’s view or evaluate Akoya eyewear.

Bagher et al. Cureus. 2023;15(12):e50023. DOI: 10.7759/cureus.50023.

Pediatric care · Broader background · 2019Virtual reality during pediatric proceduresSystematic review and meta-analysis · Immersive distraction

What was studied: Research on immersive virtual reality during pediatric medical procedures.

What was found: The review reported lower pain and anxiety with VR compared with usual care, while noting variation in study quality, procedures, and results.

How it relates: This is background on attention and distraction. VR includes immersive content and can involve sound and interaction; it does not isolate simple visual blocking. These findings cannot be attributed to Akoya’s pediatric or adult eyewear.

Eijlers et al. Anesthesia & Analgesia. 2019;129(5):1344–1353. DOI: 10.1213/ANE.0000000000004165.

Adult and younger populations · Broader background · 2021Virtual reality and fear of medical proceduresSystematic review · Different interventions and settings

What was studied: 28 studies of VR used for distraction, exposure, or hypnosis in several medical settings.

What was found: Some settings showed reduced fear, but results were unclear in others. Study quality and risk of bias limited firm general conclusions.

How it relates: The review provides broader context for research on the patient experience. Its interventions are different from Akoya eyewear, and it does not demonstrate that a visual barrier produces the same effects.

Kilic et al. Annals of Behavioral Medicine. 2021;55(11):1062–1079. DOI: 10.1093/abm/kaab016.

Our own feedback

Listening to patients

Early feedback on the adult Akoya Eye Shield came from a breast-biopsy setting. It describes patient experience and does not establish clinical effectiveness or results for the pediatric product.

Adult Akoya Eye Shield · Post-use questionnaire45 completed questionnaires among 50 patientsView the reported feedback and its limitations

Akoya’s original feedback summary reported favorable ratings for ease of use, reliability, comfort, and recommendation following use during breast-biopsy care.

Figures reported in Akoya’s feedback summary

Reading these figures: These percentages reproduce the original summary. Exact item wording, item-level response counts, and the calculation method are not provided in that summary. They should not be interpreted as clinical success rates or as the percentage of patients whose anxiety or pain improved.

What this can tell us: The feedback raises useful questions about product acceptance and the experience of wearing the adult Eye Shield. It was not randomized or controlled, and it cannot show that Akoya caused a clinical benefit. It does not evaluate Pediatric Character Eyewear.
